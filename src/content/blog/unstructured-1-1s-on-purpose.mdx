[Part 1 of these notes](/blog/one-year-into-engineering-management) ended with the team
recovering from a release they'd carried on overtime, and me upstairs
arguing that the recovery should cost the company something. That thread
has an ending now: I negotiated an extra week of PTO for every person on
the team. Not a thank-you email, not a pizza budget, a week of actual time
back for the time they gave up. The team was happy with it, and I'll take
that as a win. The honest footnote is that the better win would have been
a release plan that never needed compensating for, and that one is still
on me.

The six months since have been quieter, which turned out to mean harder in
a different way. Nothing was on fire, so the work became the slow kind:
one-on-ones, feedback cycles, and two people problems I have not solved.
Notes on all of it below.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - The overtime from the big release was compensated with an extra week
>   of PTO per team member. The team took it well; I'd rather never have
>   to negotiate that again.
> - I tried the 1-1 formats from *The Manager's Path*, then deliberately
>   dropped them. Unstructured 1-1s on a fixed every-two-weeks cadence work
>   better for me, as long as I know what I want them to cover.
> - 360 feedback got useful the moment the questions got specific. People
>   rarely volunteer observations; they answer what you actually ask.
> - Two hard cases stay open: engineers whose self-assessment runs ahead
>   of their delivery, and the "alpha geek" pattern Camille Fournier
>   describes. Nothing I've tried on the second has worked yet.

## Structured 1-1s, then deliberately not

Camille Fournier's [*The Manager's
Path*](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-managers-path/9781491973882/)
(O'Reilly, 2017) lays out a set of 1-1 styles in the chapter on managing
people: the to-do list meeting, the catch-up, the feedback meeting, the
progress report, the getting-to-know-you. When I set out to standardize
my 1-1s after the last post, I tried running them along those lines,
picking a style and holding some shape to the meeting. It felt responsible. It also, over a few months,
felt wrong for what I was trying to do, so I dropped it on purpose.

The problem wasn't the styles. It was that any imposed shape competed with
my actual goals for these meetings, which are three:

1. Connect with each person on a human level.
2. Make it easy for them to bring up whatever they want to talk about.
3. Still get across what I need to.

A structured agenda serves the third goal and quietly works against the
first two. If the meeting has a format, people bring format-shaped
updates. The thing they actually wanted to raise, the doubt about their
role or the friction with a teammate, doesn't fit the template, so it
waits, sometimes forever. Unstructured, at least for me, is the structure
that lets those things surface.

The one piece of scaffolding I kept is cadence: a 1-1 with each team
member every two weeks, no exceptions, no skipping because "nothing's
going on." For a couple of people I'm considering moving to monthly, but I
haven't yet.

Unstructured doesn't mean aimless. There's a set of things I want covered
over the course of these conversations, even if no single meeting hits all
of them:

- **Wellbeing, at the personal level.** How are you doing? How are you
  feeling? How's the work-life balance holding up? Family, interests,
  hobbies. I keep a log of each person's interests and bring things up
  once in a while. Asking about the thing someone mentioned a month ago
  does more for the relationship than any opener I could script, and the
  log means I don't have to trust my memory to do it.
- **Feedback, theirs first.** About me, about the company, about the team.
  Then I share mine. The ordering is deliberate: it's easier to hear
  feedback after you've been genuinely listened to, and easier to give it
  before you're bracing for what's coming back.
- **Career development.** Are you learning? Growing? Happy with your role?
  Anything you'd like to learn or do differently? Anything I can help
  with?

That's the whole system. A calendar cadence, a short mental checklist, and
a log of what people care about. Everything else is conversation.

## Making 360s worth the forms

Our 360-degree feedback rounds had already drifted into form-filling. Everyone
completed them, nobody learned much, and the output read like it was
written to be finished rather than to be used. My goal for this cycle was
plain: feedback that produces something the person can act on.

The fix that mattered was not more process. It was the realization that
people generally don't share much unless you ask them specific questions.
Ask "any feedback for this person?" and you get politeness. Ask about a
concrete area of their work, in terms that point at behavior rather than
character, and you get observations someone can do something with. So I
rewrote the questions to be specific and actionable, and the quality of
what came back changed noticeably.

There's a reason the generic prompt fails, and it isn't laziness. Maury
Peiperl, writing in [Harvard Business
Review](https://hbr.org/2001/01/getting-360-degree-feedback-right)
(January 2001) after ten years of research on peer appraisal, calls one of
the core tensions the Paradox of Roles: you're asking colleagues to be
peer and judge at the same time, and most people resolve that discomfort
by softening everything into mush. A specific question about a specific
behavior asks for an observation, not a verdict. That's a much easier
thing to give honestly, and my experience this cycle matched Peiperl's
research better than I expected.

## Two cases I can't close yet

Six months of steady management work, and the two hardest problems on my
desk are both people patterns. Neither has a resolution in this post,
because neither has a resolution yet.

The first: engineers whose confidence runs ahead of their current ability.
They see themselves as more capable and more technically strong than they
are right now, and it shows up in a consistent pattern: they present
themselves as the person for the hard problems, take on too much, and then
under-deliver on what they committed to. I want to be fair here, because
the raw material is genuinely good. This is ambition outrunning skill, not
a character flaw, and ambition is the part you want to keep. My struggle
is that I haven't found the way to channel it: how to size their
commitments to their present abilities without reading as a vote of no
confidence in their future ones. Everything I've tried so far either
dented the ambition or left the overcommitment intact.

The second is what Fournier calls the "alpha geek" in a sidebar of *The
Manager's Path*: the engineer who values intelligence and technical skill
above all other traits, and who believes they have all the right answers.
Her description matched what I was seeing closely enough to be a little
uncomfortable. These engineers give you a genuinely tough time. I've tried
direct feedback. I've tried walking through the effect the behavior has on
the rest of the team, concretely, case by case. So far it isn't working,
and I don't have a next move I believe in. I could dress that up as a
strategy in progress, but the accurate version is: this one is beating me
at the moment.

Both files stay open.

## Next: productivity and quality

With the 1-1 cadence settled and the feedback cycle in better shape, my
attention is shifting to the team's output: productivity, and the quality
of what we ship. It's early, and I've learned enough from the sections
above not to announce a system before it has survived contact with real
weeks. When there's something worth writing about, I'll write it.
