In April I ended [the previous post in this series](/blog/unstructured-1-1s-on-purpose)
on an open thread: engineers whose self-assessment ran well ahead of their
delivery, and the archetype Camille Fournier calls the alpha geek in
[The Manager's Path](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-managers-path/9781491973882/).
I had tried direct feedback, more than once, in more than one shape, and it
wasn't landing. I wrote that I didn't have an answer yet.

Here's the honest ending: I never found one. Over the summer I went to my
own management, asked for a change, and we parted ways with the two
people at the center of those patterns.

I want to write that carefully, because it would be easy to make it sound
like a verdict on them, and it isn't one. It was a bad fit, and a bad fit
costs everyone. They were stressed. The team's morale was sliding, and so
was mine. The one-on-ones had started to feel like the same conversation on
a loop, and a conversation that repeats without moving anything isn't
feedback anymore, it's friction. Part of this job is finding the key that
unlocks each person, and I turned every key I had on those locks. None of
them fit. Maybe a better manager finds the one I missed. I didn't, and
after enough months of trying, saying so out loud was the only honest move
left.

What I wasn't prepared for was how the aftermath felt: relief, almost
immediately, and then guilt about the relief. Both were telling the truth.
Relief, because the daily cost was real and it stopped. Guilt, because
parting ways is a failure state, and I own my share of this one. If either
of them ever reads this, I hope it reads the way I mean it: not "you were
the problem", but "the fit was the problem, and I couldn't fix the fit".
I'd stand behind that sentence with them in the room.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Some fits don't work no matter how many approaches you try, and ending
>   one can be a relief and a failure at the same time.
> - The culture-as-repetition bet from year one is paying off: openness,
>   ownership, and feedback keep improving on their own momentum.
> - Streamlined onboarding now gets new joiners productive in their first
>   couple of days instead of weeks.
> - The next frontier is turning ownership into accountability, because
>   quality still isn't where it needs to be.

## The compounding part

[In the first post of this series](/blog/one-year-into-engineering-management)
I wrote that culture is mostly repetition: you say the same small things
about how we work, over and over, until they stop being your things and
become the team's. Two years in, that bet is paying interest.

The change is visible day by day now, not quarter by quarter. People are
happier. Discussions are more open, and ideas come from more of the room,
not just the loudest corner of it. The sense of ownership keeps growing,
and with it people keep picking up responsibility I never assigned. None
of this traces back to a single initiative I can point at. It came from
the same messages, repeated, long past the point where I was bored of
saying them. That's why this post is called what it's called. Culture
doesn't add, it compounds, and the early payments were invisible.

## 360s, grown up

The 360 feedback I rolled out in year one has matured into something more
structured and more meaningful. The early rounds were me chasing forms;
these rounds run on their own weight. The specific-questions rewrite from
the spring is what stuck: what comes back now points at actual work
instead of pleasantries, and acting on it takes less pushing every cycle.
The bigger shift is on the receiving
end: people don't just give feedback more openly now, they take it without
flinching, and they act on it. That last part is the one I'd have paid for
up front if I could have. It turns out you can't buy it, you can only
repeat your way there.

## Onboarding in days, not weeks

We're growing, which turned onboarding from an occasional chore into a
recurring event. So we streamlined it, and the difference shows up in the
only measure that matters: new joiners now get productive within their
first couple of days instead of weeks. There's a culture angle here too.
A new joiner learns how the team works from what their first week feels
like, and "you shipped something by Wednesday" teaches ownership better
than any document about our values would.

## Close out, solve, or delegate

I have a habit I'm only now working on seriously: doing everything myself,
especially development, because I felt I could do it better and faster on
my own. The first half of that feeling is sometimes even true, which is
what makes it a trap. Faster-by-me is the right answer for one task and
the wrong answer for a team, because every task I keep is a task nobody
else gets better at. A manager whose team can't absorb work hasn't built
a team, just an audience.

Will Larson has a triage framing I've started applying to everything that
lands on my desk: [close out, solve, or delegate](https://lethain.com/close-out-solve-or-delegate/).
Close a thing out so that this specific ask never comes back. Solve the
category so it stops recurring at all. Or delegate it to someone who will
own it, even when, especially when, you'd be faster yourself. I've just
started working through this with my team members and the new leads,
which in practice means catching myself mid-reach for a task and asking
who should actually own it. My hit rate isn't impressive yet. The reach
is fast and old.

## Ownership needs a sibling

The team owns things now; that part worked. What I want to add next is
accountability, ownership's less comfortable sibling, and this is where I
have to disclose the asterisk on all the good news above: quality is still
a concern. Bugs keep spilling through here and there. A team that feels
ownership but doesn't yet hold itself to the outcomes will ship pride and
defects in the same release.

My working theory is that accountability closes that gap. Not blame, but
the expectation that owning a thing includes owning what it does in
production. We're working on it, and I'm deliberately not claiming it's
solved, because the last time I left a thread open in one of these posts,
resolving it took most of six months and the hardest decision of the job
so far.

Two years into the role, the one constant is that the ground keeps moving.
Get through the transition and the hard cases show up. Resolve the hard
cases and quality moves to the front. Fix quality, presumably, and
something else is already in the mail. What compounds, though, keeps
compounding through all of it. That's the part I'd tell year-one me to
trust.
