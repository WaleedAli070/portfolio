The home page of this site is now a game. You fly a small ship through an
asteroid field, and the five glowing nav nodes scattered around the screen
are the site's sections: fly close to one and its card opens with a particle
burst. One of them launches a second game, a lunar lander with limited fuel
and a terrain that regenerates on every attempt.

Both games are vanilla `<canvas>` and TypeScript. No engine, no physics
library, no sprite framework. The whole thing is five small modules:
a starfield backdrop, the deck sim, the lander sim, a shared particle
helper, and a controller that owns the canvas and the render loop.

## Navigation as gameplay

The deck is an Asteroids variant where crashing into rocks is survivable.
Rocks above a size threshold split in two when shot, smaller ones vaporize,
and the ship wraps around the screen edges. But the actual job of the game
is navigation: each nav node has an activation radius a little wider than
its drawn circle, and crossing it marks the node discovered and opens the
matching section card. A legend in the corner lists the same sections as
plain clickable text, so nobody has to earn the About page.

## The 60Hz contract

Every physics constant in these games is a per-frame value: friction is
`vx *= 0.93`, lunar gravity is `vy += 0.018` per step, thrust burns 0.24%
of the tank per frame. I tuned all of them in a design mockup on a 60Hz
display, and they only mean anything at that rate. Run the same constants
inside `requestAnimationFrame` on a 120Hz monitor and the ship handles
like it's on ice while the lander falls twice as hard.

The fix is the classic accumulator loop. The controller measures real time
between frames, banks it, and drains the bank in fixed 16.67ms simulation
steps; drawing happens once per display frame regardless of how many steps
ran. One guard matters in practice: when a background tab gets throttled
and comes back, the bank can hold seconds of debt, so the loop caps it at
100ms instead of fast-forwarding the game through everything you missed.

## Judging a landing

The lander generates its terrain as twelve line segments with one pair
flattened into a landing pad. Touchdown runs three checks: vertical speed,
horizontal drift, and tilt. Fail any of them, or miss the pad, and the
rocket disappears into a burst of red particles. The HUD shows the same
three numbers while you fly and flips them from green to red as they cross
their thresholds, so a crash never feels like the game's opinion.

A clean landing earns a celebration: a three-pixel-per-cell astronaut
climbs out, walks to a spot beside the ship, raises a pole, and unfurls a
rippling flag. The whole sequence is a hand-written timeline in frame
counts, and the success modal waits for the flag before it appears.
Interrupting the animation with a scoreboard would have wasted the best
part.

## Keeping the DOM out of the sim

The game modules never touch the page. They expose callbacks (`onNodeOpen`,
`onLanderResult`, `onCelebrate`) and the Astro page owns everything else:
the HUD text, the section cards, the boot sequence, the touch controls.
That split kept both sides simple, and it means the sim runs headless in
tests or a different shell without changes.

Visitors without JavaScript, and anyone with `prefers-reduced-motion` set,
skip the game and get a static layout with the same links. A cockpit is a
nice front door, but it stays optional.
