[Shipping the flight deck](/blog/shipping-the-flight-deck) ended on a
proud note: the home page was a game, and one of its nav nodes launched a
second game. Then I started showing it to people.

A friend opened it on a desktop, wiggled the mouse, admired the stars for
a few seconds, and clicked the About link in the corner — like a
civilized person visiting a normal website. My family did worse: they
told me it looked great, and then asked what they were supposed to *do*
with it. I had built two games and successfully hidden both of them. The
deck read as a screensaver, and the lander lived behind a button nobody
pressed. A stealth launch, in the worst possible sense.

v2 is a handful of small fixes to that, plus a way to answer the question
the whole episode raised: is anyone actually playing these?

## The ship now flies itself

The strongest hint that something is playable is watching it play. Arcade
cabinets figured this out fifty years ago — the attract mode exists
because a dark screen earns no quarters. So until you touch the controls,
an autopilot now flies the ship in lazy arcs around the asteroid field,
and a control hint fades in over the canvas. Your first key press or tap
takes the stick, kills the hint, and the autopilot stays gone for the
rest of the visit.

One rule made this trickier than it sounds: nav nodes must not unlock
while the autopilot is driving, or the site starts opening its own
section cards while you watch — a portfolio reading itself to you.
Discovery is gated on real input. The design mockup only counted the
keyboard as real input, which would have left every phone visitor stuck
in attract mode forever. Caught that one during the port, not before.

## Planets are doorways now

In v1 you reached the lander through a nav node, which is to say through
a menu. v2 connects the games in-world instead: fly into one of the
planets drifting behind the asteroid field and the screen irises down
into the lander, with that planet's terrain waiting below. Land or crash,
then thrust off the top of the screen and you climb back out into space,
right where you left it. No button, no menu — the second game is now a
place.

The loop needed one guard. After you climb out, the deck drops your ship
next to the planet you just left, and without a cooldown you would iris
straight back down on the next frame. A 150-frame grace period lets you
fly clear before planets become solid again. (An earlier build without it
was a very effective infinite-lander trap.)

## Focus mode, for the three people who came to play

Most visitors treat the deck as a front door. A few treat it as the
destination, and for them, pressing F (or the titlebar button) blurs away
the site chrome: nav legend, hints, coordinates. The score panel, the
lander telemetry, and the touch controls stay — hiding the instruments
you fly by would make focus mode a punishment. A small pill stays on
screen as the exit.

The content pages got matching stitches: the red traffic light in the
terminal titlebar now closes the page back to the deck, and a small
"◂ flight deck" pill beside it does the same in words.

## Is anyone actually playing?

Every change above is a bet that visitors will play if the games invite
them properly. I wanted the bet scored, and pageviews cannot score it:
the game *is* the home page, and thanks to the autopilot it now plays
itself, so a visit proves nothing about a visitor.

The standard answer is an analytics suite, and I refuse to give it. As a
user I spend half my browsing life (exaggerating, I know, but you get
the point) dismissing cookie banners, and I am not inflicting one on you
to count two arcade games. I have also spent enough years around
security products to know how much data the average site collects
against how little it uses — over-collection is the default, not the
exception, and the only reliable way out is to not collect. And this
site has a standing rule that has served it well: no dependency without
a clear need. Google Analytics to increment two counters fails that test
by a comfortable margin.

The site already runs on Cloudflare Workers, so the whole apparatus is
one small worker and a KV namespace. The page fires a beacon at exactly
two moments:

- **Deck play**: your first control input of the page load, keyboard or
  touch. The autopilot does not count — watching is not playing.
- **Lander play**: entering the lander after you have interacted, whether
  through a planet descent or the mode button. The autopilot occasionally
  drifts into a planet on its own; those descents are excluded too.

Each fires at most once per page load, via `navigator.sendBeacon`, in
production builds only. The worker validates the game name and bumps two
KV keys: a running total and a per-day key like `deck:d:2026-08-11` that
expires after sixty days. `GET /api/plays` returns the whole picture as
JSON, which makes my analytics dashboard a URL I, or even you, can curl
whenever the curiosity hits. KV writes are not atomic, so two
simultaneous players can cost me an increment — at this site's traffic,
that error is smaller than the noise I add by testing the game myself,
so the counter stays simple on purpose.

The tally is anonymous by construction: no cookies, no identifiers, no
fingerprinting, no third-party script. Sixty lines of worker, twenty
lines in the page, and nothing for a consent banner to confess.

The counters went live today, so the scoreboard reads zero as I write
this. Ask me in a month whether the deck or the lander wins. My money is
on the lander — crashing is more fun than navigating.
