It's been just over a year since I moved from individual contributor to
[engineering manager](/about), and the honest word for it is rollercoaster. The
title changed once; the job changed several times. For most of this year
I've been the EM, the software architect, a developer, and the DevOps
function at the same time, and we just saw a major migration and release
through with fewer people than it needed. Chaotic days. Great learning.

Linda Hill spent a decade studying star performers who become first-time
managers, and her conclusion in
[Becoming the Boss](https://hbr.org/2007/01/becoming-the-boss) is that
most of them struggle at first because being in charge turns out to be
nothing like what they imagined. That matched my year closely enough to
sting. So this post is field notes, not advice from someone who has it
figured out. I'm one year in, I've paid tuition, and these are the
receipts.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Not saying no cost me more in my first six months than any technical
>   decision I made.
> - Culture isn't announced. It's repeated, again and again, until it
>   holds without you in the room.
> - If you only fight for someone once they have an offer in hand,
>   you've already lost them.
> - Structure in 1-1s and feedback matters most exactly when the manager
>   and the team are new to each other.

## Saying no

The hardest part of this year wasn't architecture, hiring, or shipping.
It was the word no. I didn't say it, not to stakeholders, not to scope,
not to requests that arrived sideways, and I paid a heavy price for that
in the first six months.

Part of it was the four hats. When you're also the architect, the
developer, and the DevOps person, every yes lands on your own calendar,
and the calendar doesn't negotiate. But the deeper part was me. New
managers want to prove they can handle it, and "I'll take that on" feels
like leadership right up until the moment everything is late at once.
The lesson wasn't that other people asked for too much. It's that I made
commitments my capacity couldn't honor, and that's a manager failure,
not a workload one.

## Culture is repetition

You cannot establish culture in a day, a week, or a month, and
definitely not while architecture reviews, production issues, and
deployments are competing for the same hours. And you cannot do it by
saying it. Announcing a value in a meeting does nothing. You have to do
the thing, then do it again, and again, until people trust the pattern
more than the announcement.

The value I care most about is ownership. We're fortunate to work in an
industry full of smart people; give them real ownership and they can do
wonders. I watched it happen this past year. When the release got hard,
nobody waited to be handed the problem in front of them. They stepped up
and made it happen. I can't take credit for their talent, but I'll take
a little for insisting, repeatedly, that problems belong to the people
closest to them.

## The engineer who left

Our best engineer resigned just before the most critical stretch the
company has had. He'd been offered a better package elsewhere, and he
took it. No drama, no bad blood. He made a sensible decision for his
career, and I'd probably have made the same one in his position.

Could we have matched the offer? Arithmetically, yes, if we'd let two
other engineers go to free up the payscale. I wasn't willing to trade
two people for one, whatever the one was worth. And I don't believe the
match works anyway. My working philosophy: once someone has made up
their mind and has an offer in hand, a counter buys you maybe six
months. They've already pictured themselves gone. If you value someone
that much, the time to show it is long before they start to think about
leaving. Pay, growth, interesting problems, all of it, early.

The uncomfortable footnote is that at a smaller company some of this is
simply out of your control. There are packages we cannot match, and no
1-1 cadence changes that. What I can control is whether people leave
over things I could have fixed. I don't think he did. But I'll never
fully know, and that's part of the tuition too.

## Structure I should have built sooner

My 1-1s this year were unstructured: random conversations, whatever was
on top of either of our minds. Same with feedback, which I gave when
something prompted it and skipped when nothing did. When you're new to
management and the team is new to you, that looseness feels friendly and
low-pressure. It's actually a disservice. People deserve to know where
they stand on a schedule, not by accident.

Two changes came out of that. First, I'm standardizing 1-1s and feedback
for the team: a regular cadence and a consistent shape, so nobody has to
guess. One habit from the loose era survives, though. I still recommend
team members read Peter Drucker's
[Managing Oneself](https://hbr.org/2005/01/managing-oneself) and take
honest stock of their own strengths. It's the piece I point new joiners
to first.

Second, and bigger: we recently rolled out a 360-degree feedback
mechanism across the company and tied it to the appraisal cycle. There
was no process before. Appraisals rode largely on the impression a
person's senior had formed, which is a polite way of saying vibes. I'd
been through 360s at my previous company and found the signal genuinely
useful, so I pushed for it here. It's not a cure-all, and done
carelessly 360s can fail in well-documented ways
([Maury Peiperl catalogued the paradoxes](https://hbr.org/2001/01/getting-360-degree-feedback-right)
back in 2001), but a structured mechanism with multiple inputs beats one
person's mood on review day.

## Feedback that doesn't land

An open problem, filed honestly: sometimes people don't take feedback
well, and I haven't figured out how to reliably make them see that it's
in their favor. The feedback can be accurate, specific, and kindly
meant, and still land as an attack. I used to assume that was a
receiving problem. A year in, I think it's mostly a delivery problem,
which makes it mine. I'm working on it: more context, better timing,
asking more than telling. No tidy resolution here. Ask me next year.

## Morale, and an open thread

If there's one thing I did reasonably well this year, and I'll allow
myself this one, it's that the team stayed motivated through a stretch
that gave them every reason not to be. We were short on time and short
on people for this release, and they worked overtime under real pressure
for a significant period. Morale held anyway. Not because of any trick.
I tried to stay visible in the work, honest about the state of things,
and loud about whose wins these were.

But motivation through a crunch is a debt, not a payment. The team
earned more than thanks, and right now I'm discussing with the
higher-ups how to compensate them properly for what they put in. No
commitments yet, and I won't pretend the conversation is an easy one.
Let's see how it goes.
